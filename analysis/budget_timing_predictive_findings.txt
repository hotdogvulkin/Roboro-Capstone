ANALYSIS 1. PRIOR-YEAR AUTOCORRELATION — is lateness sticky?
==============================================================================

-- correlation of days-late with the prior observation -----------------------
  strict lag-1 fiscal year   n=460  Pearson r=+0.584 (p=2.1e-43)   Spearman rho=+0.677 (p=5.9e-63)
  consecutive budget events  n=518  Pearson r=+0.593 (p=2.1e-50)   Spearman rho=+0.692 (p=4.4e-75)

-- confusion matrix on strict lag-1 pairs (late = days_late > 0) -------------
                        this year LATE   this year ON TIME
  last year LATE                   32                  26     (32/58 = 55.2% repeat)
  last year ON TIME                27                 375     (375/402 = 93.3% stay clean)

  chi-square = 102.2 (p=5.1e-24),  odds ratio = 17.1 (Fisher p=9.7e-18)
  lift over the 13.0% base rate: 4.2x

-- is it momentum, or is it just the state? (within-state demeaning) ---------
  observed within-state r          -0.141  (n=460)
  permutation null                 -0.089 +/- 0.064
     (demeaning with the state's own mean induces a negative bias by construction;
      the permutation null measures exactly how much, so it can be subtracted off)
  z vs null = -0.80,  two-sided permutation p = 0.427

  between-state share of variance  65.1%   (F=19.7, p=1.9e-89)
  intraclass correlation (ICC)     0.62

-- walk-forward forecast — only years BEFORE the target are used -------------
  (evaluated on the 418 state-years with at least 3 years of prior history)

  A: late last year                    precision  53.6%   recall  52.6%   accuracy  87.3%
  B: late in >50% of prior years       precision  77.1%   recall  47.4%   accuracy  90.9%
  C: late in >=1 prior year            precision  31.0%   recall  77.2%   accuracy  73.4%
  D: never predict late (null model)   precision   nan%   recall   0.0%   accuracy  86.4%

-- miss rate by state --------------------------------------------------------
  Massachusetts    12/12 = 100.0%
  Wisconsin         6/6  = 100.0%
  Pennsylvania      9/12 =  75.0%
  North Carolina    5/7  =  71.4%
  New York          9/13 =  69.2%
  Oregon            7/13 =  53.8%
  New Hampshire     2/6  =  33.3%
  Rhode Island      4/13 =  30.8%
  Ohio              2/7  =  28.6%
  Hawaii            3/13 =  23.1%
  ... 28 of 50 states never missed once.

  >> FINDING: Lateness is highly persistent, but the persistence lives in the state, not in the year: 65% of the variance in days-late is between states (ICC 0.62), and once a state's own average is removed, last year's result carries no information about this year's (permutation p=0.43). A state that missed last year misses again 55% of the time versus a 13% base rate (4.2x lift) — but its multi-year track record is the sharper instrument still, forecasting a miss at 77% precision against 54% for last year's outcome alone.

ANALYSIS 2. ELECTION-YEAR EFFECT — do legislators hurry home to campaign?
==============================================================================

-- unpaired comparison (confounded — see the caveat below) -------------------
  subset                           election yrs                      off-years     MWU p
  all states       n=261 mean  -46.8d late 10.7%   n=307 mean  -31.9d late 15.0%    0.0006
  annual cycle     n=199 mean  -42.9d late 10.1%   n=200 mean  -37.8d late 12.5%    0.3311
  biennial cycle   n= 62 mean  -59.3d late 12.9%   n=107 mean  -20.8d late 19.6%    0.0000
  full-time leg.   n=101 mean  -22.3d late 20.8%   n=109 mean   -0.8d late 36.7%    0.0004
  part-time leg.   n=160 mean  -62.3d late  4.4%   n=198 mean  -49.0d late  3.0%    0.0045

  CAVEAT: for a biennial state with odd-year sessions, 'off-year' and 'the long
  budget-writing session' are the same thing, so the unpaired biennial gap is not an
  election effect. The paired and fixed-effects tests below deal with this.

-- paired within-state (each state compared against itself) ------------------
  election year, all states          states= 41  mean  -16.1d  median   -8.3d  faster 30/41  Wilcoxon p=0.0001
  election year, annual states only  states= 31  mean   -8.5d  median   -4.5d  faster 21/31  Wilcoxon p=0.0066
  PLACEBO: even calendar year        states= 41  mean  -14.2d  median   -4.5d  faster 30/41  Wilcoxon p=0.0007
  The placebo lands close to the real thing, and it has to: for 44 of 50 states an
  election year IS an even year, so the two dummies are nearly the same variable. The
  fixed-effects model below is what separates them.

-- OLS with state AND session-year fixed effects -----------------------------
  election coefficient  -11.19 days   (se 4.77, t=-2.35, p=0.019, n=568)
  Year fixed effects absorb everything national — COVID, revenue cycles, and any
  generic even/odd-year rhythm. What identifies the coefficient is the six states
  whose legislative election calendar differs from the other 44: AL, MD (midterm
  4-year terms) and LA, MS, NJ, VA (odd-year elections).

-- states that break the pattern ---------------------------------------------
  faster in election years  Oregon             -66.6d vs   +38.8d   (-105)
  faster in election years  North Carolina      +1.3d vs   +77.5d   (-76)
  faster in election years  Maine              -74.7d vs   -18.7d   (-56)
  faster in election years  Washington         -81.0d vs   -29.2d   (-52)
  SLOWER in election years  Rhode Island       +15.4d vs    +4.2d   (+11)
  SLOWER in election years  Utah               -94.6d vs  -107.2d   (+13)
  SLOWER in election years  Massachusetts      +43.3d vs   +19.8d   (+24)

  >> FINDING: Election years really are faster, by roughly a week to a week and a half. Within annual-cycle states the budget lands 8.5 days earlier on average in a legislative election year (21 of 31 states move in that direction, Wilcoxon p=0.007), and the effect survives state and year fixed effects at -11.2 days (p=0.019). It is a real but second-order signal: it shifts the expected date, and does not rescue a chronically late state.

ANALYSIS 4. SESSION OVERRUN — does a session running long predict a late budget?
==============================================================================
  Source: NCSL's 'Legislative Session Calendar', read at two vintages of the same
  document — an early-season edition giving the SCHEDULED adjournment and a
  post-session edition giving the ACTUAL sine die. Editions come from NCSL and from
  the Internet Archive, so the scheduled column is a contemporaneous forecast rather
  than a date reconstructed after the fact. See build_session_overrun.py.
  410 state-years with both dates and a matching budget observation.


-- correlation of session overrun with days-late -----------------------------
  n = 410 state-years, 44 states, 12 session years
  overrun: mean +5.6d, median +0.0d, ran long in 29% of state-years
  Pearson  r   = +0.136  (p=0.0058)
  Spearman rho = +0.160  (p=0.0012)
  restricted to exactly-dated scheduled adjournments: n=360, r=+0.131 (p=0.013)
  dropping the 28 sessions that adjourned >14d early: n=382, r=+0.147 (p=0.0039), rho=+0.176 (p=0.00056)

-- overrun bands -------------------------------------------------------------
  on or before schedule    n=290  mean  -50.2d  missed   6.6%
  1-13 days over           n= 62  mean  -41.5d  missed   6.5%
  14-29 days over          n= 20  mean  -47.5d  missed   0.0%
  30+ days over            n= 38  mean  -24.6d  missed  13.2%

  ran long vs adjourned on schedule: miss rate 7.5% vs 6.6%
  Mann-Whitney on days-late p=0.011;  odds ratio 1.2 (Fisher p=0.83)

-- does overrun add anything beyond knowing which state it is? ---------------
  within-state (demeaned) r = +0.245  (p=5.9e-07, n=407, 42 states)
  This is the question that matters for early warning: given the same state, does a
  session that runs longer than usual land its budget later than usual?
  permutation null +0.001 +/- 0.052   -> p < 0.001 (z = +4.7)

  OLS with state fixed effects:  +0.246 days later per day of overrun
    (se 0.051, t=+4.81, p=2.2e-06, n=410)
    -> a session running 30 days past its scheduled adjournment implies a budget
       roughly 7 days later than that same state's own norm.

-- robustness of the fixed-effects slope -------------------------------------
  all state-years                            n=410  +0.246 (se 0.051, p=2.2e-06)
  excluding 2021 (redistricting returns)     n=369  +0.328 (se 0.072, p=6.4e-06)
  excluding COVID years 2020-2021            n=347  +0.323 (se 0.102, p=0.0017)
  excluding overruns beyond 90 days          n=401  +0.252 (se 0.088, p=0.0045)
  trimmed to (-14, +90] days                 n=373  +0.376 (se 0.120, p=0.0019)
  The slope gets steeper, not shallower, once those are removed.

-- coverage limits -----------------------------------------------------------
  6 states never appear: Massachusetts, Michigan, New Jersey, New York, Ohio, Wisconsin
  Most are missing because their legislature has no scheduled adjournment at all —
  NCSL prints '*' for them. An overrun cannot be defined where nothing was scheduled.
  Of those, 6 are full-time legislatures: Massachusetts, Michigan, New Jersey, New York, Ohio, Wisconsin

  >> FINDING: Session overrun is a genuine leading indicator, and the only one of the four that a platform could act on while the outcome is still in play. It is strongest exactly where a forecaster needs it — inside a single state, comparing a session against that state's own norm: within-state r=+0.24 (p=6e-07), and state fixed effects put the slope at +0.25 days of budget delay per day of overrun (p=2e-06), so a month-long overrun implies a budget about 7 days later than usual. State-years that ran 30+ days long missed the fiscal-year start 13% of the time against a 7% rate across this sample. Its blind spot is structural: the measure is undefined for the year-round legislatures that miss most often, because they never schedule an adjournment to overrun.

ANALYSIS 5. PARTY DIRECTION — is lateness a red/blue story?
==============================================================================

-- Raw comparison, no controls -----------------------------------------------
             states   miss rate   mean days  full-time legs
  R              23        4.7%       -56.2         2/23   
  D              16       22.4%       -21.8        10/16   
  divided        11       24.9%       -19.1         6/11   

  Kruskal-Wallis, all three groups   p = 0.014
  Mann-Whitney R vs D, miss rate     p = 0.002
  Mann-Whitney R vs D, mean days     p = 0.008

-- The confound --------------------------------------------------------------
  share of each group with a full-time legislature:
    R             8.7%
    D            62.5%
    divided      54.5%

  Legislature type is the strongest structural variable in this dataset
  (analysis 1 and the on-time gap below), and it is distributed across party
  groups about as unevenly as it could be. Party here is close to a restatement
  of professionalism, so the raw contrast above cannot be read as a party effect.

-- OLS: miss rate ~ legislature type + party  (state level, n=50) ------------
  intercept                    +2.31  (se  4.83)   p = 0.634
  full-time legislature       +27.42  (se  7.95)   p = 0.001  <-- significant
  party = D                    +2.90  (se  8.60)   p = 0.737
  party = divided              +7.65  (se  9.16)   p = 0.408

  >> FINDING: Party direction does not survive the control. Republican-trifecta states miss 4.7% of budgets against 22.4% for Democratic trifectas (p = 0.002), but 62.5% of Democratic-trifecta states have full-time legislatures against 8.7% of Republican ones. In one model legislature type is worth +27 points (p < 0.001) and party is worth +2.9 and nothing statistically (p = 0.74). Do not pitch a party story; it falls apart on the first informed question.

  CAVEAT: trifecta status is a 2026 snapshot applied to a 13-year record. Several
  states changed control inside the window, so this measure is weak on its face
  even before the confound. That weakness cuts against the raw result, not the null.

ANALYSIS 7. THE STRUCTURAL BASELINE — legislature type, and the four nulls
==============================================================================

-- Legislature type ----------------------------------------------------------
                states   on-time   mean days   never missed
  full-time         18     66.1%        -8.4         5/18   
  part-time         32     95.9%       -53.1        23/32   

  Mann-Whitney, on-time rate   p = 0.0003
  Mann-Whitney, mean days      p = 0.0005

-- The nulls, each tested against the same per-state miss rate ---------------
  state size (log population, controlling for legislature type)   p = 0.91
  budget cycle (annual n=31 vs biennial n=19)                 p = 0.27
    (the same comparison pooled over state-years instead: p = 0.049 — clustering ignored, do not quote)
  per-capita spending level (Spearman vs miss rate)             p = 0.09
  party control, divided vs unified                            p = 0.95

-- The interaction that does bite --------------------------------------------
  full-time  + divided   n= 6   mean miss rate  45.7%
  full-time  + unified   n=12   mean miss rate  28.0%
  part-time  + divided   n= 5   mean miss rate   0.0%
  part-time  + unified   n=27   mean miss rate   4.8%

  >> FINDING: Legislature type is the structural variable. Part-time legislatures enact on time 96% of the time against 66% for full-time ones (p = 0.0003), and close 45 days earlier on average (p = 0.0005). State size (p = 0.91), budget cycle (p = 0.27), spending level (p = 0.09) and party control (p = 0.95) are all null. Divided government only converts into missed deadlines where the calendar never forces adjournment.

==============================================================================
                        WHAT TO PUT IN THE WHITE PAPER                        
==============================================================================
1. BUDGET TIMING IS A STATE TRAIT, NOT A YEARLY COIN FLIP.
   65% of the variance in how late a budget lands is variation between states
   rather than within them (ICC 0.62). 28 of 50 states have never missed their
   fiscal-year start once in the panel; Massachusetts has missed all 12. Knowing
   which state you are looking at is most of the forecast.

2. THE TRACK RECORD BEATS LAST YEAR.
   A rule that fires when a state has missed in a majority of its prior years calls
   the next miss at 77% precision against a 13% base rate. "Was it late last
   year?" manages only 54% — and once each state's own average is removed, last
   year's result carries no information at all (permutation p=0.43). Recency is
   noise; history is signal.

3. SESSION OVERRUN IS THE ONE LIVE, IN-SEASON SIGNAL.
   Within a state, every day a session runs past its scheduled adjournment adds about
   0.25 days to the budget's delay (p=2e-06) — a month-long overrun means a budget
   roughly 7 days later than that state's own norm. This is the only one of the
   analyses that moves while the outcome is still undecided, and it is exactly the kind
   of thing a platform tracking legislative calendars in real time can watch.

   Secondary: legislative election years pull budgets about 9 days earlier in
   annual-cycle states (p=0.007; +27 days under state and year fixed effects,
   p=0.019).

RULED OUT — and worth saying so, because each is a plausible story that fails:
   * fiscal stress          real per-capita spending growth vs days-late, r=+0.05 (p=0.47);
                            contracting states enacted EARLIER, not later
   * national revenue cycle  NASBO general fund revenue growth vs the national on-time
                            rate, no relationship across FY2015-FY2027
   * year-to-year momentum   no within-state autocorrelation once the state's own
                            average is removed

==============================================================================
                                 METHOD NOTES                                 
==============================================================================
p-values are computed live by this script from scipy 1.15; nothing is hardcoded.
Tests are two-sided throughout. Mann-Whitney U and Wilcoxon signed-rank are used in
place of t-tests wherever the days-late distribution is the skewed one it plainly is;
where a mean is reported a Welch or OLS check was run alongside and agreed on sign.

The unit of analysis is the state-year for correlations and the state for paired tests.
Because two thirds of the variance in days-late sits between states rather than within
them (analysis 1), any comparison that pools state-years without a within-state control
will pick up state composition and report it as an effect. Analyses 2, 3 and 4 each
therefore carry a demeaned or fixed-effects version, and those are the ones to quote.
